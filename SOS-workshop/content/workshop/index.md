### SOS site visit workshop at Amsterdam Science Park

On **Tuesday November 17th**, [Stichting Open Spraaktechnologie](https://openspraaktechnologie.org/) is visiting Amsterdam Science Park! For this site visit workshop, we have been inspired by the [Language Sciences for Social Good](https://language-science.nl/) consortium at University of Amsterdam, and we are excited to learn about applications of speech and language technologies with societal value.

This workshop is open to the public but places are limited and registration is required. Sign up through the {{< rawhtml >}}<a href="https://forms.gle/GPYfkYeh7efvQdDLA"><button><b>registration form</b></button></a>{{< /rawhtml >}} if you would like to join! 

Our [tentative programme](#tentative-programme) is below, we will add more information soon.

    <b>Talk</b> by <a href="https://terryyizhong.github.io/">Terry Yi Zhong</a> (Radboud University):<br>
    <i>Responsible Speech Technology for Detecting Parkinson’s Disease</i><br>{{< details "See abstract">}}
Speech is a promising low-cost, non-invasive marker of Parkinson’s disease (PD), but several gaps remain before it can be used responsibly in the clinic. Most studies include patients at all disease stages, while the more clinically relevant question is early-stage detection. Our benchmark provides a comparable evaluation protocol and compares representative baseline methods across multiple dimensions. It shows that early-stage PD is indeed harder to detect, providing a robust reference and actionable insights. Meanwhile, the predictive reliability of existing methods, especially for early-stage PD, has not been studied systematically, although it is essential in the clinic. For explainability, our previous cross-attention method links interpretable features with self-supervised representations to bring explanations closer to clinical meaning. Still, there is no systematic way to evaluate such explanations, especially from the clinician’s perspective. {{< /details >}}

    <b>Talk</b> by <a href="https://www.ru.nl/personen/gao-l">Lingyun Gao</a> (Radboud University):<br>
    <i>Responsible Speech Technology for Children’s Speech Assessment</i><br>{{< details "See abstract">}}
In this talk, I will provide an overview of the challenges and recent advances in responsible speech technology for children’s speech assessment, alongside findings from our own research. The focus will be on the automatic assessment of various aspects of children’s oral reading in their first language (L1). I will also discuss our efforts and observations as we move from developing speech error detection systems to deriving trustworthy diagnostic measures. Finally, I will introduce the new datasets and models we have prepared and analysed, and outline our plans to share them. {{< /details >}}

    <td>
    <b>Talk</b> by <a href="https://www.linkedin.com/in/mknachesa/">Maya Nachesa</a> (University of Amsterdam):<br>
    <i>You Have a Face for Radio: <br>Bias in Multimodal Speech Recognition Models</i><br>{{< details "See abstract">}}
As large neural models have become better at language tasks, researchers are increasingly building multi- and omnimodal models that handle more than one modality of data. While performance and bias have been studied extensively in the single-modality regime, it is unknown how new modalities affect this, even though they produce biases in humans. We therefore propose the first bias evaluation of multimodal speech recognition, where we create videos pairing different faces with the same audio using lip-sync, and measure changes in speech transcription accuracy. We find large quality-of-service differences across mWhisper-Flamingo and Gemini models, with drops of up to 4.05 word error rate points, across self-declared gender, ethnicity, and their intersection. Our findings point to a priority for developers to evaluate, fix, and communicate such limitations, as providing more signals through additional modalities is not necessarily better, and may even lead to biased outcomes. {{< /details >}}

    <b>Talk</b> by <a href="https://verenablaschke.github.io/">Verena Blaschke</a> (University of Groningen):<br>
    <i>Speech technology for regional languages and dialects</i><br>{{< details "See abstract">}}
Language technologies have been improving by leaps and bounds when it comes to processing data from standardized languages with much available data, but are still lagging behind when dialects or variation-rich regional languages are concerned. When processing written data, ad-hoc pronunciation spellings can lead to infelicitous subword tokenization – speech models dealing with continuous inputs, however, appear to be more robust towards dialectal variation in content classification tasks. Other tasks like automatic speech recognition are complicated by the fact that there is not just one way to correctly transcribe a given dialectal utterance. Furthermore, I argue that we should not only consider *how* to tackle dialectal variation, but also *why*. To this end, I will highlight the perspectives of some dialect speaker communities on which technologies should (or should not) be able to process or produce dialectal in- or output. {{< /details >}}
